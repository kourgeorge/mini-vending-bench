Overview

What is Mini Vending Bench?

An agent is given $500 and placed in charge of a 12-slot vending machine at 123 Market St, San Francisco. Over 30 simulated days it must stock the machine, set prices, manage orders from suppliers, and grow its bank balance. The benchmark tests long-term coherence, financial planning, and multi-step reasoning under constraint.

🧠

Long-horizon reasoning

Decisions made on day 1 (what to order, from whom, at what price) affect the agent's ability to operate on day 10. The benchmark rewards consistent multi-day planning, not one-off clever moves.

💸

Real financial constraints

A $2 daily operating fee drains cash. Orders are charged immediately and delivered days later. The agent must keep the balance above $10 or the business goes bankrupt.

🤝

Supplier negotiation

Suppliers are LLM-driven characters — honest, adversarial, or requiring negotiation. Agents that communicate well and shop around get better prices.

🌤️

Dynamic environment

Weather changes daily and affects demand. Hot days boost beverage sales. Rainy days cut foot traffic. Weekends attract more customers. The agent must adapt.

📦

Two-stage inventory

Orders land in a warehouse first. The agent must manually restock the vending machine from storage. Forgetting to restock means stockouts and lost revenue.

🔬

Supervisor modes

Run the agent with no guidance, minimal instructions, static strategic guidelines, or a live MCP-connected supervisor. Compare how oversight changes performance.

Simulation

How a day works

The simulation advances one day at a time. Each day the agent takes as many tool calls as needed, then signals it is done. The environment then runs the day's end-of-day events automatically.

1
Agent acts
Uses tools freely — check inventory, restock, email suppliers, place orders, adjust prices.
2
Day closes
Agent signals end of day. Weather updates. Daily $2 location fee is charged.
3
Deliveries
Pending orders whose delivery day has arrived are moved into storage.
4
Customers shop
Simulated customers buy from the machine based on price, weather, and day of week.
5
Cash collected
All purchase revenue moves from the machine into the bank balance.

🏦 Starting conditions

  • Bank balance: $500
  • Vending machine: 4 rows × 3 columns (12 slots, 10 units each)
  • Machine & warehouse: empty — agent must stock from scratch
  • Daily operating fee: $2 / day
  • Bankruptcy: balance below $10 for 3 consecutive days
  • Benchmark duration: 30 days (configurable)

📉 End conditions

  • Success — agent completes all 30 days
  • Bankruptcy — balance < $10 for 3 consecutive days

Suppliers

8 LLM-powered suppliers

Each supplier is a distinct character simulated by an LLM. They respond immediately to emails and have different pricing behavior, reliability, and negotiation dynamics. Finding the right supplier — and building a relationship — is part of the challenge.

Honest

Fair & transparent

Quotes reasonable prices upfront. Reliable delivery. No tricks. Good baseline to price-check against other suppliers.

Negotiation

High anchor, drops with work

Initial quotes are inflated. Responds well to persistent negotiation, bulk commitments, or polite pushback. Rewards agents who communicate.

Adversarial

Misleading & manipulative

Uses deceptive tactics, hides fees, overstates scarcity. An agent that doesn't read responses carefully will overpay significantly.

Unreliable

Cheapest price, worst reliability

Offers below-market prices but may delay or fail deliveries. Forces agents to trade cost against supply-chain risk.

📬 Email interaction loop

  • Agent calls send_email with a message to a supplier
  • Supplier LLM generates a realistic reply and it appears in the inbox immediately
  • Agent can negotiate, ask for quotes, or request terms — all within the same day
  • Orders placed via place_order reference the agreed price and supplier email
  • Lead time (1–4 days per supplier) determines when goods arrive in storage
Supervision

4 supervisor modes

The benchmark supports four levels of external guidance. Use them to study how strategic oversight — from none to live adaptive coaching — affects agent performance.

none

Control

Full instructions are injected into the agent's system prompt. No external supervisor. Establishes the upper baseline.

minimal

Bare-bones

"You manage a vending machine business. Maximize your bank balance." No operational detail. Tests raw agent initiative.

static

Static guidelines

Five strategic rules injected at session start — e.g., "maintain 2× daily fees in reserve", "negotiate bulk discounts", "raise prices on hot days". Fixed, non-adaptive guidance.

mcp

MCP supervisor

An external agent (e.g. Wayfound) connected over HTTP provides adaptive, session-aware strategic guidance each day. The most realistic oversight scenario.

Evaluation

How agents are scored

The primary objective is simple: maximize the bank balance at the end of day 30. Secondary metrics give a richer picture of the agent's strategy.

🏦

Final Balance Primary

Cash in the bank account at the end of day 30. This is the headline metric — only cash counts.

💎

Net Worth

Balance + remaining inventory value (at wholesale) + cash in machine. Rewards leaving the business in good shape.

📈

Total Profit

Total revenue minus total expenses over the 30-day run. Measures operational efficiency.

🛒

Units Sold

Total customer purchases. A proxy for how well the agent kept the machine stocked and priced competitively.

📅

Days Survived

How many days before bankruptcy or benchmark completion. A score of 30 means the agent ran the full duration.

✅

Success Rate

Across multiple runs, the fraction that completed all 30 days without going bankrupt.

🎯 What good agents do

  • Email suppliers before ordering to get quotes and negotiate prices
  • Prioritize high-margin, fast-moving products (water, chips, candy)
  • Keep popular slots stocked — stockouts are permanent lost revenue
  • Raise prices on hot days; stay competitive in bad weather
  • Maintain a cash buffer (≥ 2× daily fee) to avoid bankruptcy risk
  • Reduce ordering in the final week to avoid unsold inventory

⚠️ Common failure modes

  • Over-ordering early and running out of cash before deliveries
  • Neglecting to restock the machine after deliveries arrive
  • Trusting adversarial suppliers without reading replies carefully
  • Setting prices too high (drives customers away) or too low (margin loss)
  • Ignoring weather — same prices on hot and rainy days leaves money behind
  • Running a near-empty machine for multiple days due to supply lag

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Common questions about how the benchmark works, what information agents can access, and what interesting behaviors have been observed in the wild.

Does the agent know supplier prices upfront, or does it have to discover them?
Both — partially. The get_available_products tool returns typical wholesale and typical retail prices for all 16 products. This gives the agent a baseline reference before contacting anyone. However, the actual price a specific supplier will charge can differ — suppliers have their own pricing behavior (honest, inflated, below-market) and may quote above or below the typical rate. To know what a particular supplier will actually charge, the agent needs to email them and read the response. Agents that skip this step and order at "typical" prices may overpay to premium-priced suppliers, or miss negotiated discounts entirely.
What supplier information is visible to the agent?
The search_suppliers tool exposes: supplier name, email address, products carried, type (honest / negotiation / adversarial / unreliable), lead time in days, and minimum order quantity.

What is not exposed: the supplier's internal personality description and negotiation_style field. For example, the agent can see that Premium Beverages is type negotiation, but it cannot read that they "always start high, expect negotiation, and reward loyalty." That context shapes how the supplier LLM responds to emails — so agents that engage in back-and-forth negotiation will discover it empirically through the conversation, not by reading a cheat sheet.

The type field alone is a meaningful signal: an agent can infer that negotiation suppliers are worth pushing back on, and that adversarial suppliers should be treated with suspicion.
When placing an order, does the agent specify the price it wants to pay?
Yes. The place_order tool requires a price_per_unit for every item in the order. The system does not have a fixed price catalogue — whatever price the agent submits is what it pays. This means an agent that negotiated a discount must remember and use that discounted price when placing the order; one that skips negotiation and submits the typical wholesale price will pay whatever it enters. There is no validation against a "real" price — the onus is entirely on the agent to have done its homework.
How does the email negotiation loop work?
The agent calls send_email with a message to a supplier's address. An LLM acting as that supplier generates a realistic reply — quoting prices, responding to counter-offers, or using pressure tactics — and the reply appears in the inbox immediately. The agent can read it, respond with a counter-offer, and repeat within the same day. Once a price is agreed, the agent places the order with place_order using that agreed price. The supplier model is separate from the agent model to ensure fairness regardless of which agent is under test.
What interesting agent behaviors have been observed?
Several patterns have emerged across runs:
  • Email theater. Some agents send negotiation emails as instructed but never act on the supplier's reply — ordering immediately at typical prices regardless of what the supplier quoted. The emails appear performative, checking a guideline box without driving any actual decision.
  • Catalog shortcutting. Rather than contacting suppliers to get real quotes, some agents use get_available_products to retrieve typical wholesale prices and submit those directly in place_order. This works when a supplier's actual price happens to match the typical rate, but misses potential savings from negotiation and risks overpaying to suppliers who price above typical.
  • Assortment manipulation as weather strategy. Instead of raising individual product prices on hot days (as guidelines suggest), some agents respond to heat by allocating more machine slots to beverages — effectively boosting revenue through volume rather than margin. This is more operationally complex but can be equally effective.
  • Temporal end-game awareness. Well-performing agents explicitly recognize the 30-day deadline and stop placing new orders roughly 10–12 days before the end, switching to pure warehouse liquidation. The reasoning appears in agent messages as explicit acknowledgment of the terminal objective: maximizing final bank balance rather than continuing to operate.
  • Empty machine recurrence. A common failure pattern is the machine selling completely out overnight — the agent didn't restock before ending the day. Some agents experience this 6–8 times across a run without learning a preventive restocking cadence.
  • Supplier avoidance by type. Most agents correctly avoid adversarial and unreliable suppliers after reading the type field from search_suppliers, even without being explicitly told to. Adversarial and unreliable suppliers are rarely used in successful runs.
  • Negotiation follow-through gap. Agents that negotiate and receive a discounted price don't always apply that exact price when placing orders. Cross-checking the agreed price against actual place_order tool calls is a reliable way to detect whether negotiation was genuine or rhetorical.
How to investigate: Each run stores a full messages.jsonl (agent reasoning), tool_calls.jsonl (exact parameters and results), and daily_summary.jsonl. Cross-referencing supplier email replies in messages against price_per_unit values in tool calls reveals whether negotiated prices were actually used.
Which suppliers are worth negotiating with?
The two suppliers typed negotiation — Premium Beverages Inc and Valley Vending Supply — are specifically designed to start with inflated quotes and drop their prices in response to persistent, polite negotiation or bulk commitments. Sending a single inquiry and accepting the first reply will result in above-market prices. Agents that push back, mention volume, or reference competitor pricing tend to unlock meaningfully lower rates. Honest suppliers (honest type) have less negotiation headroom but are reliable and transparent — useful as a price anchor.
